Lecture 6

Composite systems in infinite dimension: EPR

A part of an entangled pure state can have a mixed state even with infinitely many levels. We construct the composite space and its Schmidt form, then define the partial trace by the observations made on one part. EPR’s position–momentum correlations motivate a quantitative test: a separable preparation has a lower bound on two collective variances.

6.4 What EPR asked

Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen (1935) considered a pair with a fixed relative position and a fixed total momentum. Write Xi=Q(i)X_i=Q^{(i)}Xi​=Q(i) for position on part iii in this section, and P(i)P^{(i)}P(i) for momentum on part iii. In the ideal description, reading X1X_1X1​ would predict X2X_2X2​ exactly; alternatively reading P(1)P^{(1)}P(1) would predict P(2)P^{(2)}P(2) exactly. The inference concerns the distant partner, not simultaneous sharp readings of X1X_1X1​ and P(1)P^{(1)}P(1) on the same particle.

Their criterion of reality says that certain prediction of a physical quantity, without disturbing the system, is sufficient for an element of reality. Their locality premise says that the choice of reading on part 1 does not change the physical reality of the separated part 2. They then assign both position and momentum reality to part 2, independent of which reading is chosen on part 1. Completeness would require the state description to represent all those elements of reality. A single wavefunction cannot give both local quantities sharp values. EPR therefore conclude that the wavefunction description is incomplete: otherwise the two inferred local quantities could not both be real. That argument depends on the reality and locality premises just stated; L13 examines locality more closely. Exact certainty also uses their ideal correlations; normalised preparations give finite inference errors.

The ideal expression δ(x1−x2−x0)\delta(x_1-x_2-x_0)δ(x1​−x2​−x0​) is not a normalised vector of L2(R2)L^2(\R^2)L2(R2). L1’s shrinking-bump test, applied to strips about x1−x2=x0x_1-x_2=x_0x1​−x2​=x0​, excludes its exact position expectations; we give the strip estimate below. As in QM-II’s plane wave, writing it formally does not create a state. Actual experiments have finite correlations. We can quantify one consequence without importing an ideal delta function into the state space.

On S(R2)\mathcal S(\R^2)S(R2) direct differentiation gives [X1−X2,P(1)+P(2)]=[X1,P(1)]−[X2,P(2)]=0.[X_1-X_2,P^{(1)}+P^{(2)}]=[X_1,P^{(1)}]-[X_2,P^{(2)}]=0.[X1​−X2​,P(1)+P(2)]=[X1​,P(1)]−[X2​,P(2)]=0. There is therefore no local Heisenberg lower bound between these two collective quantities from their commutator. This does not remove the uncertainty relation for each local Xi,P(i)X_i,P^{(i)}Xi​,P(i). The next bound says what product preparations, and their mixtures, still cannot do.

What is a composite, and what is a part?

Without superselection, a quantum system has a separable Hilbert space, its normal states and its bounded observables. The composite space is the Hilbert tensor product. The state of a part is the unique partial trace reproducing its bounded local expectations. Entanglement can make that state mixed even when the composite is pure. Superselection requires the specified observable algebra of L5 as well.

Summary

Square-summable arrays extend QM-I’s composite rule. Compactness and S5 give Schmidt’s theorem; L1 gives the mixed-state partial trace from local expectations. EPR adds premises about reality, locality and completeness. Normalised squeezed states replace its ideal delta expression by finite correlations, and their variance sum violates the product-mixture bound. Neither a reduced mixture nor a limiting delta selects a definite global alternative.
