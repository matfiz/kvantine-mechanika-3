Lecture 12

The measurement problem

What this lecture does

An instrument gives the probabilities and conditional states of a reading. A unitary apparatus model explains those statistics, but does its global output describe one definite result? We prove a calibrated pointer cannot turn a superposition into one pointer sector or a global pointer mixture. A three-qubit test identifies what decoherence leaves open, and the premises C, U and D organise the possible responses.

12.1 Calibration does not produce one global outcome

Work within one coherent sector with the full bounded-observable algebra, so that the global interference test below is admitted, rather than excluded by an additional restriction of the kind specified in L5. On HS\Hil_SHS​ let (Ej)(E_j)(Ej​) be a complete countable system PVM, with strong sum 11\Id11. Let (Πj)(\Pi_j)(Πj​) be orthogonal pointer projections on a separable meter space HM\Hil_MHM​, and let φ0\varphi_0φ0​ be its fixed unit ready vector. A unitary UUU is calibrated if, for every unit ψj∈ran⁡Ej\psi_j\in\operatorname{ran}E_jψj​∈ranEj​, χj=U(ψj⊗φ0)∈ran⁡(11⊗Πj).\begin{equation} \label{eq:12:calibration} \chi_j=U(\psi_j\otimes\varphi_0) \in\operatorname{ran}(\Id\otimes\Pi_j). \end{equation}χj​=U(ψj​⊗φ0​)∈ran(11⊗Πj​).(12.1) Thus every input already belonging to sector jjj gives the corresponding pointer reading with certainty. The output system need not remain ψj\psi_jψj​, and the measured sector may be degenerate. Calibration concerns all its vectors. Here calibration on an ONB already implies that by bounded linearity and the closed pointer range; calibrating only selected vectors without a spanning argument is insufficient.

12.2 Three premises that do not fit together

For the ideal pointer in this argument, a definite outcome jjj means a state supported in its pointer sector; ignorance of an already definite outcome permits a mixture of such states. That is the state–outcome identification being used. A diagonal marginal alone is not the global mixture required by it.

Definition 12.2 (The measurement trilemma). The three premises in this calibrated state-and-pointer description are:

  • C: the global quantum state is complete; there are no further actual physical facts selecting a result beyond that state.

  • U: the same universal unitary dynamics governs the system, apparatus and any included environment.

  • D: each run ends with one unique, observer-independent global outcome, represented by the specified definite pointer alternatives.

Corollary 12.3. For the calibrated pure-ready model, an input with two nonzero spectral components, and the stated pointer-sector identification of definite outcomes, C, U and D cannot all hold.

With C and U, Theorem 12.1 leaves the global superposed state, not a state of one of those alternatives or its ignorance mixture. D demands an additional account. The definition is a conditional organisation of these premises, not a theorem refuting every interpretation of the word “outcome”. In particular, a definite outcome relative to a branch is not D’s unique global outcome.

The operational calculus remains usable. Given a recorded result, an instrument supplies its conditional state and later probabilities. The theorem asks how a description with universal U and complete C accounts for that unique record in the first place. Renormalising a projected branch is a probability-conditioning rule; it is not by itself a unitary derivation of a stochastic physical selection.

Part V follows the three responses discussed by Maudlin (1995) and Tumulka (pp. 93–94). Bohmian mechanics adds configuration facts beyond the wavefunction, giving up C in this sense. GRW changes the unitary dynamics, giving up U. Everett retains the unitary state and takes outcomes relative to branches, giving up D’s unique global outcome. These are the premises changed; their separate locality and empirical commitments must also be stated, not decided by this taxonomy. Other interpretive proposals make other specifications. We will compare the three on the same questions in L14.

A fundamental restriction of the observable algebra is another substantive specification, as L5 showed. Simply declaring pointer cross blocks unobservable would change the algebra under discussion; it is not a consequence of a small environmental overlap within the same full algebra. Practical inability to perform a global test and a fundamental prohibition of that test are different physical premises.

Coherent access to every record is essential. If the environment record stays untouched and is traced out, no observable on system plus meter can recover the lost cross block. For a macroscopic environment that access may be extraordinarily difficult. Its difficulty explains the success of effective classical records, while leaving the theorem’s logical distinction intact.

One can express the test as an erasure operation. Undo the two CNOTs in reverse order. The coherent state becomes ∣+x,0,0⟩\ket{+x,0,0}∣+x,0,0⟩, whose system +x+x+x probability is one. Erasing and recombining all records is a different subsequent experiment from reading and preserving their alternatives.

12.4 What moving the cut preserves

A von Neumann chain includes more parts in the unitary description: system, pointer, environment, further records. In the three-qubit example, reading the meter after the first CNOT gives jjj with probability 1/21/21/2 and conditional system state ∣j⟩ ⁣⟨j∣\proj j∣j⟩⟨j∣. Copying that retained record to the environment gives ∣jjj⟩\ket{jjj}∣jjj⟩. Keeping both interactions coherent and conditioning the final meter or environment record gives the same probabilities and conditional state. This is the restricted sense in which the cut can move without altering predictions.

More generally, write an instrument’s record isometry (L10) as Jψ=∑jKjψ⊗∣j⟩J\psi=\sum_jK_j\psi\otimes\ket jJψ=∑j​Kj​ψ⊗∣j⟩. Later controlled copying that leaves ∣j⟩\ket j∣j⟩ unchanged preserves its sector weight ∥Kjψ∥2\norm{K_j\psi}^2∥Kj​ψ∥2 and its conditional system output. Orthogonality removes cross terms from every reading that ignores or preserves those records. Positive spectral sums give the same statement for density inputs. L14 proves this identity for the fully specified two-reading capstone.

The qualification about retained records matters. A projection update used to represent actual branch selection destroys global coherence; a coherent record-erasing unitary can detect its absence. Cut independence holds for the specified record-preserving operations. An early projection-and-mixture description and a coherent late description predict different results for a record-erasing test. The inverse-CNOT calculation explicitly distinguishes those operations. In ordinary reading chains the retained-record statistics agree; that agreement does not establish a unique global outcome.

12.5 Wigner’s friend and the scope of further no-go claims

Take the meter in (12.2) as the friend’s memory; Wigner’s interference test is (12.3). Extend the chain to that memory. A unitary calibration gives a superposition of “system 0, record 0” and “system 1, record 1”. Conditional on either record, the friend assigns a definite relative result. An external observer retaining coherent control of the entire isolated laboratory instead describes its global superposition and could in principle test its interference. The choice of a record reading versus a coherent global test must be included in the experimental description; they are not one unchanged experiment.

Frauchiger–Renner (2018, arXiv:1604.07422) combine (Q) quantum probability-one inferences, (C) transfer of another agent’s certainty, and (S) exclusion of contradictory single outcomes for an agent, in a scenario with coherent external control of entire labs. They argue that these premises are incompatible. Their inference-transfer condition labelled C is not our completeness C. This is a conditional argument about the stated inference rules and quantum-control scenario; no additional theorem is proved here and no unconditional inconsistency of quantum probability is inferred.

Bong et al. (2020, arXiv:1907.05607) formulate a different bundle called local friendliness: absoluteness of observed events, no superdeterminism of the later choices, and locality of the specified response probabilities. With suitable quantum control on the friend’s scale, quantum predictions violate inequalities following from those premises. Observed-event absoluteness does not preassign every unperformed experiment; their locality premise is not Bell factorisation with outcome independence silently added. The two named arguments and this lecture’s C/U/D theorem have distinct hypotheses. Listing those hypotheses is more informative than calling each one “a proof that observers disagree”.

The physics

Proietti et al. (2019, arXiv:1902.05080) implemented an extended Wigner-friend scenario with a six-photon encoding of systems and records and tested a Bell-type inequality. Such photonic implementations probe controlled quantum memories under the corresponding premises. They do not demonstrate coherent manipulation of human observers or settle the interpretation of experience. The ideal global-control assumption is explicit in the mathematical scenario.

Does a unitary pointer end in one reading?

An instrument predicts probabilities and conditional states along a specified calibrated chain, with retained-record statistics independent of the cut. A calibrated unitary with a pure ready state does not turn a superposition into one definite pointer sector or a global sector mixture. C, U and a unique observer-independent global outcome D cannot all hold under that definite-pointer description. Decoherence explains local loss of interference but does not supply the missing selection.

Summary

The superposition proof and its nonzero global cross block establish the pure-ready obstruction. Mixed-probe and robust extensions have additional cited hypotheses. A three-qubit global test distinguishes coherence from an incoherent mixture even with identical local marginals. Moving the cut preserves the stated retained-record statistics, not arbitrary record-erasing experiments. Part V compares specified changes to C, U and D.
