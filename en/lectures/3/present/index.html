Lecture 3

Probabilities on effects

Probabilities on all effects, additive whenever the effects sum to at most the identity, are exactly density operators. Probabilities on projections alone have the same characterisation in dimension at least three, but not for a qubit. The quadratic trace rule permits pair interference and forces an ideal three-path interference combination to vanish.

3.1 From expectations to probabilities

Theorem 1.6 started with a positive linear expectation rule on B(H)B(\Hil)B(H) and obtained a density operator. Linearity already related the means of noncommuting operators. Could a rule assigning probabilities to possible readings force that linearity? The answer depends on the events to which probabilities are assigned and on which sums must be respected. Throughout, H\HilH is a nonzero separable complex Hilbert space, and the observable algebra under discussion is all of B(H)B(\Hil)B(H).

Definition 3.1 (Effect and generalised probability). An effect is a bounded self-adjoint operator FFF with 0≤F≤110\leq F\leq\Id0≤F≤11. A generalised probability is a map μ:{F:0≤F≤11}→[0,1]\mu:\{F:0\leq F\leq\Id\}\to[0,1]μ:{F:0≤F≤11}→[0,1] such that μ(11)=1\mu(\Id)=1μ(11)=1 and μ(∑kFk)=∑kμ(Fk)whenever Fk≥0,∑kFk≤11.\begin{equation} \label{eq:3:additivity} \mu\Bigl(\sum_kF_k\Bigr)=\sum_k\mu(F_k) \quad\text{whenever }F_k\geq0,\quad\sum_kF_k\leq\Id. \end{equation}μ(k∑​Fk​)=k∑​μ(Fk​)whenever Fk​≥0,k∑​Fk​≤11.(3.1) The countable clause applies when the positive partial sums converge strongly to some F≤11F\leq\IdF≤11; it does not assume a general convergence theorem for arbitrary series. Each summand is then an effect, since 0≤Fk≤F≤110\leq F_k\leq F\leq\Id0≤Fk​≤F≤11. Finite sums are included.

Every projection is an effect, but an effect need not square to itself. For example, F=12∣0⟩ ⁣⟨0∣F=\tfrac12\proj0F=21​∣0⟩⟨0∣ describes a yes outcome that occurs half the time for ∣0⟩\ket0∣0⟩ and never for the orthogonal ∣1⟩\ket1∣1⟩. One may realise this by an ideal test followed by independent random rejection of half its yes results. A general account of such readings comes in Lecture 9. At present an effect is the bounded operator to which a probability will be assigned, not an assertion that its only eigenvalues are zero and one.

The sum condition is stronger than additivity on orthogonal projections. Let Ez=(11+σz)/2E_z=(\Id+\sigma_z)/2Ez​=(11+σz​)/2, Ex=(11+σx)/2E_x=(\Id+\sigma_x)/2Ex​=(11+σx​)/2. Although they do not commute, the effects F1=Ez/2F_1=E_z/2F1​=Ez​/2, F2=Ex/2F_2=E_x/2F2​=Ex​/2 satisfy F1+F2≤11F_1+F_2\leq\IdF1​+F2​≤11, since Ez,Ex≤11E_z,E_x\leq\IdEz​,Ex​≤11. Thus our rule requires μ(F1+F2)=μ(F1)+μ(F2)\mu(F_1+F_2)=\mu(F_1)+\mu(F_2)μ(F1​+F2​)=μ(F1​)+μ(F2​). The remainder 11−F1−F2\Id-F_1-F_211−F1​−F2​ is also an effect. An operational model is to toss a fair coin, read σz\sigma_zσz​ on heads and σx\sigma_xσx​ on tails, and record outcome 1 for (heads, plus), outcome 2 for (tails, plus), and outcome 3 otherwise. In QM-I’s language these outcomes have effects F1,F2,11−F1−F2F_1,F_2,\Id-F_1-F_2F1​,F2​,11−F1​−F2​. Assigning an effect the same probability whatever procedure realises it is precisely the extra consistency premise. This does not mean that the two sharp spin components have been read simultaneously. It states a consistency requirement for probabilities on these three effects, rather than simultaneous definite values of the two spin observables.

There is also a noncontextuality assumption in assigning one number μ(F)\mu(F)μ(F) to each effect: its probability is independent of which complete family of outcomes contains it. Together with the sum rule, this is a substantive premise, not a consequence of the word “probability” alone. For a density operator the proposed assignment is μ(F)=Tr⁡(ρF)\mu(F)=\Tr(\rho F)μ(F)=Tr(ρF). The next theorem shows that these are all the assignments under the stated assumptions, including for a spin one half.

The extension steps required only finite additivity. In finite dimension this would already suffice, because every positive linear normalised functional is normal there. The proof has therefore named exactly the additional assumption that makes its infinite-dimensional conclusion possible.

3.3 Gleason, and why a qubit escapes

Suppose probabilities are assigned only to projections, with μ(11)=1\mu(\Id)=1μ(11)=1 and countable additivity only on orthogonal families. This does not explicitly relate noncommuting effects. In most dimensions it still has a remarkable consequence, but the proof is much deeper than the extension argument just given.

Theorem 3.4 (Gleason cited). On a separable complex Hilbert space of dimension at least three, every countably additive probability on projections is μ(E)=Tr⁡(ρE)\mu(E)=\Tr(\rho E)μ(E)=Tr(ρE) for a unique density operator ρ\rhoρ.

This is Landsman’s Theorem 4.29, p. 120. We use its statement, not an unwritten proof. Its probability rule assigns the same number to a projection in every orthogonal resolution containing it. That shared assignment across different bases supplies much of the theorem’s force.

In C2\C^2C2 every nonzero proper projection has the Bloch form E(n)=(11+n⋅σ)/2E(\mathbf n)=(\Id+\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)/2E(n)=(11+n⋅σ)/2, with ∣n∣=1|\mathbf n|=1∣n∣=1. Its only nonzero orthogonal partner is 11−E(n)=E(−n)\Id-E(\mathbf n)=E(-\mathbf n)11−E(n)=E(−n). Hence any function h:S2→[0,1]h:S^2\to[0,1]h:S2→[0,1] satisfying h(−n)=1−h(n)h(-\mathbf n)=1-h(\mathbf n)h(−n)=1−h(n) defines a projection probability: assign μ(E(n))=h(n)\mu(E(\mathbf n))=h(\mathbf n)μ(E(n))=h(n), μ(0)=0\mu(0)=0μ(0)=0, μ(11)=1\mu(\Id)=1μ(11)=1. A two-valued example will be useful in Lecture 13. For a unit n\mathbf nn, inspect (nz,ny,nx)(n_z,n_y,n_x)(nz​,ny​,nx​) in that order and put h0(n)=1h_0(\mathbf n)=1h0​(n)=1 if its first nonzero entry is positive, and zero if that entry is negative. Different assumptions define different classes of assignments. This is the dimension-two escape that the Kochen–Specker discussion in Lecture 13 must retain.

3.4 Which events obey Boolean rules?

For projections, E∧GE\wedge GE∧G denotes projection onto the intersection of their ranges; E∨GE\vee GE∨G denotes projection onto their closed linear span. The complement is 11−E\Id-E11−E. The projections with these operations are called the projection lattice. The general existence of projections onto closed subspaces is the Hilbert projection theorem, cited in Linear Algebra, Lecture 13. Our computations below either construct the needed projection explicitly or take place in C2\C^2C2; they do not depend on that general cited theorem.

If EG=GEEG=GEEG=GE, then EGEGEG is self-adjoint and (EG)2=EG(EG)^2=EG(EG)2=EG, with range exactly ran⁡E∩ran⁡G\operatorname{ran}E\cap\operatorname{ran}GranE∩ranG. For instance, in a three-dimensional basis take E=diag⁡(1,1,0)E=\operatorname{diag}(1,1,0)E=diag(1,1,0) and G=diag⁡(0,1,1)G=\operatorname{diag}(0,1,1)G=diag(0,1,1). The physics

Sinha and collaborators tested a three-slit arrangement in 2010 and reported a bound below 10−210^{-2}10−2 of the expected two-path interference scale for the third-order contribution. The experiment includes a background measurement with all slits closed. Real slits approximate the ideal filters: changing an aperture can also change propagation and boundary conditions. Interpreting a residual therefore requires control of those changes and detector backgrounds, rather than treating any nonzero raw combination as a failure of Born’s rule. The paper is Science 329, 418–421, doi:10.1126/science.1190545.

The assumptions making the mathematical test exact are now visible: the preparation, evolution and detector are held fixed; the filters add as orthogonal projections; and probabilities use the same original trial count. A different unitary for each slit configuration is outside this identity. This is how a theorem becomes a proposed experimental comparison: its idealisation must be checked along with its numbers.

Probabilities and states

A countably additive probability on all effects, additive on every positive family summing to at most the identity, is exactly Tr⁡(ρ ⋅)\Tr(\rho\,\cdot)Tr(ρ⋅). On projections alone, Gleason gives this conclusion in dimension at least three; a qubit admits other assignments. Commuting event projections obey Boolean rules; the full collection need not. An ideal self-adjoint observable’s events are its spectral projections; together with those of an incompatible observable they need not form a Boolean algebra. The quadratic trace rule forces I3=0I_3=0I3​=0 for the specified ideal path-filter protocol.

Summary

Busch derives linearity from the effect sum rule, then uses countable additivity to reach L1’s normal states. Gleason reaches the same trace rule from fewer events but needs dimension at least three. Neither theorem derives its premises from probability alone. The event counterexample separates incompatible contexts, and the path calculation turns the probability law into a testable ideal identity. Next we ask which changes of rays preserve their transition probabilities.
