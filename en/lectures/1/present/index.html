Lecture 1

What a state is

A state assigns expectations to observables. In a separable Hilbert space, positivity, normalisation and countable additivity on orthogonal events make this rule a density operator. This includes infinitely many populated levels, but excludes an exactly sharp position.

1.1 A rule for expectations

In QM I, a preparation chose a unit vector ψk\psi_kψk​ with probability pkp_kpk​. Every bounded observable AAA then had mean ∑kpk⟨ψk,Aψk⟩\sum_k p_k\inner{\psi_k}{A\psi_k}∑k​pk​⟨ψk​,Aψk​⟩. The density matrix collected those means into Tr⁡(ρA)\Tr(\rho A)Tr(ρA) (Definition 3.3 and Theorem 3.4); different preparations could give the same matrix. QM II treated a particle on a line by unit vectors of L2(R)L^2(\R)L2(R) and closed with a question: what replaces the density matrix when the space has infinitely many dimensions?

Start with the means themselves. We work on a nonzero separable complex Hilbert space H\HilH: it has a finite or countable orthonormal basis (ONB). Write B(H)B(\Hil)B(H) for its bounded operators. A bounded observable is a self-adjoint member of this space. An event is an orthogonal projection EEE; its expectation is its probability. Even for an unbounded observable, such as position QQQ, its spectral projections EQ(I)E_Q(I)EQ​(I) belong to B(H)B(\Hil)B(H). Knowing their probabilities determines the distribution of its readings. Its mean may still be infinite or undefined.

It is convenient to assign a complex number to every bounded operator: write A=C+iDA=C+\ii DA=C+iD with C=(A+A†)/2C=(A+\adj A)/2C=(A+A†)/2, D=(A−A†)/(2i)D=(A-\adj A)/(2\ii)D=(A−A†)/(2i) self-adjoint, and extend the expectation by complex linearity.

Definition 1.1 (An expectation rule). An algebraic state is a complex-linear map ω:B(H)→C\omega:B(\Hil)\to\Cω:B(H)→C such that ω(B)≥0\omega(B)\geq0ω(B)≥0 for every positive operator BBB, and ω(11)=1\omega(\Id)=1ω(11)=1. The letter ω\omegaω denotes an expectation rule, never a frequency; our angular frequency is ν\nuν. Positivity means that a nonnegative observable has a nonnegative mean. Linearity is a requirement on expectations, including those of noncommuting observables; it does not assert that they have simultaneous values. We will add one continuity requirement before calling such a rule a state.

1.2 Positive trace class

The finite-dimensional trace is a sum of diagonal entries. In infinite dimension a positive operator’s sum may diverge: the identity has one on every diagonal entry. We first build the part of trace theory needed for states. The two standing facts used here are S5, the spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators, and S6, existence of a positive square root. These are allowed facts under the course’s proof convention.

Definition 1.3 (Positive trace class and density operators). A positive bounded operator ρ\rhoρ is positive trace class if, for one ONB (ek)(e_k)(ek​), ∑k⟨ek,ρek⟩<∞\sum_k\inner{e_k}{\rho e_k}<\infty∑k​⟨ek​,ρek​⟩<∞. This sum is its trace, Tr⁡ρ\Tr\rhoTrρ; for a divergent positive sum write Tr⁡ρ=+∞\Tr\rho=+\inftyTrρ=+∞. A density operator is a positive trace-class operator with Tr⁡ρ=1\Tr\rho=1Trρ=1.

The words “for one ONB” are enough: the next proposition proves that the sum is independent of the basis. A positive operator of infinite trace is not trace class. The infinite value is basis independent too: a finite sum in another basis would, by the proposition, make every basis sum finite.

Proposition 1.4 (The trace and its spectral form). Every positive trace-class operator is compact. It has an orthonormal eigenbasis (φj)(\varphi_j)(φj​) with eigenvalues pj≥0p_j\geq0pj​≥0, and ρ=∑jpj∣φj⟩ ⁣⟨φj∣,Tr⁡ρ=∑jpj=∑k⟨ek,ρek⟩\begin{equation} \label{eq:1:trace} \rho=\sum_j p_j\proj{\varphi_j},\qquad \Tr\rho=\sum_jp_j=\sum_k\inner{e_k}{\rho e_k} \end{equation}ρ=j∑​pj​∣φj​⟩⟨φj​∣,Trρ=j∑​pj​=k∑​⟨ek​,ρek​⟩(1.2) for every ONB (ek)(e_k)(ek​). The operator series converges in operator norm. For a density operator, the pjp_jpj​ sum to 1.

For a density operator, this decomposition supplies one possible preparation; it does not single out how the state was prepared. Lecture 2 studies that freedom.

1.3 Normal states are density operators

If (Ek)(E_k)(Ek​) are mutually orthogonal event projections, their sum is an operator defined by its action: ∑kEkψ=lim⁡N→∞∑k≤NEkψ\sum_k E_k\psi=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{k\leq N}E_k\psi∑k​Ek​ψ=limN→∞​∑k≤N​Ek​ψ for each ψ\psiψ. Its limit acts as the identity on each range and as zero on their common orthogonal complement, hence is the projection onto their closed span. This is a strong sum. Convergence need not hold in operator norm: for basis truncations ENE_NEN​, ENψ→ψE_N\psi\to\psiEN​ψ→ψ but ∥11−EN∥=1\norm{\Id-E_N}=1∥11−EN​∥=1 in infinite dimension. The bound in Lemma 1.2 alone cannot pass to such a limit.

Definition 1.5 (Normality). An algebraic state is normal if every countable orthogonal family of projections satisfies ω(∑kEk)=∑kω(Ek).\begin{equation} \label{eq:1:normal} \omega\Bigl(\sum_k E_k\Bigr)=\sum_k\omega(E_k). \end{equation}ω(k∑​Ek​)=k∑​ω(Ek​).(1.5) In this course, state means a normal algebraic state.

Normality is countable additivity of probabilities for exclusive events. In particular, if an ONB exhausts the possible alternatives, their probabilities must sum to 1. It rules out assigning probability zero to every one of those alternatives while assigning probability one to their union. It is an assumption about the expectation rule, not about any particular preparation.

In Cn\C^nCn, an orthogonal family has at most nnn nonzero projections, so linearity already implies normality. The theorem recovers QM I’s density matrices from rules for expectations, without first choosing an ensemble. A ray is included: ρ=∣ψ⟩ ⁣⟨ψ∣\rho=\proj\psiρ=∣ψ⟩⟨ψ∣ for unit ψ\psiψ gives ω(A)=⟨ψ,Aψ⟩\omega(A)=\inner\psi{A\psi}ω(A)=⟨ψ,Aψ⟩. On an infinite-dimensional space the extra countable-additivity clause is essential. This is not peculiar to position space: every separable infinite-dimensional Hilbert space is unitarily equivalent to L2(R)L^2(\R)L2(R), by sending one ONB to another. The counterexample below therefore transfers to any such space.

What is a state? First answer

A state is a positive, normalised linear rule for bounded expectations, countably additive on orthogonal events. On B(H)B(\Hil)B(H) for separable H\HilH, it is exactly ω(A)=Tr⁡(ρA)\omega(A)=\Tr(\rho A)ω(A)=Tr(ρA) for one density operator. A sharp event EA(I)E_A(I)EA​(I) has probability Tr⁡(ρEA(I))\Tr(\rho E_A(I))Tr(ρEA​(I)). Unbounded means require an additional convergence statement. Neither a preparation ensemble nor a measurement update is specified by this definition.

Summary

Finite positive trace forces compactness, and the trace is basis independent. Positivity and Cauchy–Schwarz reconstruct an operator from rank-one expectations; normality makes its trace one and recovers all bounded expectations. The thermal oscillator is an infinite-rank example. An exactly sharp position cannot be represented by any density operator. Next we ask how many preparations one state can have.
